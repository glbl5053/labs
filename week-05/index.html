Week 05 · Workshop

Calling models directly.

Step behind the chat window: send a request to a model yourself, put one prompt to nine models side by side, and then look at how a model like these is built. No coding experience is expected.

What is an API?

An application programming interface (API) lets one program ask another program to do something.

When you use a chat app, the app writes a request on your behalf and shows you part of the reply. The request names a model, carries your words, and often carries instructions you never see. The reply includes the answer along with a count of what was used and what it cost.

Calling the API means writing that request yourself. It is how every app, agent, and research pipeline that uses a language model talks to it. Once you have seen one request, you have seen the shape of all of them.

An API key is the pass that says which account pays. Treat it like a password: keep it out of shared documents, screenshots, and GitHub. Yours is a practice key funded by the course with a spending cap, so it cannot touch your bank account, your Yale login, or your computer. If you share it by accident, replace it and tell us.

Why bother

Three things the chat window does not show you.

  1. The model is a line of text you can change. Swap one ID and the same question goes to a different company's model. Through one broker, OpenRouter, the course key reaches several hundred of them.
  2. Every answer has a price. You are billed per token in and per token out. Between the models we use today, the price of output differs by a factor in the hundreds.
  3. There is no memory in the model. A conversation is a list of messages that is sent again in full on every turn. What an app calls “memory” or “context” is something the app adds to that list.

What happened next

The numbers keep going down

Karpathy's small training setups became public test beds for how quickly a model can be trained. Two things have happened on them since, and both matter more for policy than the models themselves.

1 · People made it faster · the speedrun

From 45 minutes to under 40 seconds

In May 2024, Karpathy's GPT-2 training code reached a set quality target in 45 minutes on eight H100 GPUs. Keller Jordan turned that into an open competition: same hardware, same target, fastest code wins. By the 92nd record, on August 30, 2026, the time was 0.665 minutes, and the amount of training text needed had fallen from 10 billion tokens to under 330 million. Nobody bought a faster computer. The gains came from dozens of contributors changing the model's design, the training method, and the code.

See the record table →

Each of these measures one score on a fixed test set for a small model, and recent speedrun records include techniques tuned to that benchmark. Read them as evidence of how fast methods improve and of who, or what, is now doing the improving. They are not a forecast for large models.

Why this is in a policy course

You will not build a model. You will have to judge claims about them.

It gives you a mental model that fits in your head. The first objective of this course is a working picture of how these systems produce their output. The small GPT in the video does the same basic thing as the models you called in Part 1: predict the next token, given the ones before. Last week's tiny network was too simple to be convincing and today's largest are too big to inspect. This one sits between them.

It gives the vocabulary something to refer to. Parameters, tokens, training data, context window, compute: these words appear in model cards, vendor pitches, export rules, and draft legislation. After watching someone set each of them by hand, you can tell when a document uses one precisely and when it is waving at it.

Cost and access are policy variables. A training result that took 45 minutes in 2024 takes under one minute on the same machines two years later, and the price ladder in Part 1 spans a factor of several hundred today. Rules written around who can afford to train or run a model age at that speed. So do arguments that assume only a few actors ever can.

Agents are built from this. In the coming weeks you will direct AI agents. An agent is a program that makes the API call from Part 1 in a loop, reading each reply to decide the next request. The autoresearch graph above is what one of those loops produced overnight. Knowing the single call makes the loop much less mysterious.

It calibrates your trust. Seeing a tiny model and a frontier model answer the same question, and seeing where each goes wrong, is better preparation for deciding what to delegate than any general advice about “AI.”

How to watch as a non-programmer. Skip the syntax. For each section, try to finish the sentence “this part exists so that the model can …”. Note every number he chooses by hand, such as how many layers, how much text, how long to train. Those choices, scaled up, are what separate the rungs of the ladder in Part 1.
